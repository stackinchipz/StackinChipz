Executive summary

Tesla Megapod Program: Tesla as offtaker at Bitcoin-miner and neocloud sites November 8, 2026

The ask

Approve a 90-day program to sign the first three site agreements under which Tesla deploys Megapods (Tesla compute plus Megapack 3 storage in a self-contained block) at third-party sites that already have power, and pays the host a fixed fee per reserved megawatt. Target: 150–300 MW under signed term sheet by day 90, 400–600 MW energized by the end of 2027, 1 GW by the end of 2028.

Why

  1. Tesla needs the compute and cannot wait for the grid. Tesla runs more than 225 MW of training compute at Giga Texas and targets 400 MW by year-end 2026. The next 400 MW on Tesla's own interconnection is a 2029 event: ERCOT's large-load queue is 474 GW, US time-to-power averages four years, and large transformers take 30–48 months.
  2. Miners own the exits. Roughly 20 GW of energized or interconnection-approved capacity sits with Bitcoin miners and power-first developers whose mining margins have collapsed (Bitcoin ~$76,000, hashprice at a five-year low, one in five legacy miners mining at a loss). Hyperscalers and AI labs have signed about $130B of leases with them since mid-2024; Tesla is not yet among the buyers.
  3. Tesla has something no other tenant has. It makes the battery every one of these sites needs to firm a curtailable interconnection under Texas SB6 and ERCOT's batch rules, and that battery is sold out for 2027. It can also show up with the data hall built in, which removes the $10–12M-per-megawatt shell from the deal. That is why Tesla can pay roughly half the market rent and still be the host's best offer.
  4. The sellers are motivated right now. CoreWeave's bonds yield above 16% and Microsoft says it will use fewer neoclouds. Nscale lost three Microsoft commitments. Crusoe sold a 1.8 GW Wyoming campus for lack of a tenant. Fermi America faces lender deadlines this month with 1.5 GW of turbines and no closed lease. Keel Infrastructure has 2.2 GW in development and zero leases. Galaxy has an 830 MW approved site, $180M of long-lead gear bought, and no tenant. MARA promised the market a lease by year-end.

The offer

Tesla is the tenant. The host delivers energized megawatts at the point of interconnection and keeps the land, interconnection, utility relationship, and site operations. Tesla funds and owns everything from the switchgear in and pays a site fee on reserved gross megawatts: open at about $0.75M per MW-year, close at $0.9–1.05M, walk away above $1.25M. Ten-year term with two five-year options, Tesla parent guarantee, removable equipment. Power passes through at cost. For hosts that need it, Tesla adds a priority Megapack allocation, battery-as-a-service, warrants (as Google, AMD, and Nvidia have taken from these same companies), or a guarantee the host's lenders can finance against.

For the host that is three to five times current mining margin with no construction loan and rent starting in months. For Tesla it is 1 GW three years sooner than a greenfield build, with no stranded shell, roughly 2 GWh of internal Megapack pull-through, and grid diversification across five ISOs. The 10-year cash cost is about neutral against building; the program is a purchase of time and optionality while AI5, AI6, and Terafab mature.

Where to start

Seven Tier 1 targets with energized, uncontracted ERCOT and SPP capacity and named commercial leads: Riot (Rockdale, ~500 MW still mining), Cipher Digital (Odessa 207 MW; 1.4 GW curtailable pipeline), Core Scientific (~385 MW leasable), Galaxy (Helios II 830 MW), Bitdeer (Rockdale 563 MW, "too big for us"), Hut 8 (Vega and West Texas sites; batteries already in the Beacon Point design), and MARA (Central Texas and Matagorda). Tier 2 adds IREN, TeraWulf, CleanSpark, Keel, Soluna, Fermi, Applied Digital, Lancium, Prometheus, and Cormint. Tier 3 is the neoclouds: Nebius and Nscale first, because both have asked publicly for partners who finance, build, and operate.

The package includes the strategy, the economics with deal comps, a 28-company target list with per-company hooks, a contacts file of 109 named executives at 34 companies with sourced titles (no guessed emails), full call scripts, a five-touch email sequence plus situational emails, objection handling for hosts and for Tesla's own leadership, an indicative term sheet in two variants, and a 90-day plan with milestones and kill criteria.

What has to be true inside Tesla first

A named sponsor on the Energy side and the AI side (Energy leadership changed in October 2026). A Megapack carve-out of roughly 2 GWh over 2027–28 that does not come out of signed customer backlog. A frozen reference-block datasheet. A fee model with approval thresholds. Legal clearance on the "Megapod" name given an existing third-party mark. A firewall from SpaceX and xAI: this program is Tesla-only.

Principal risks

Host credit (hashprice lows, converts due 2030–32, going-concern language at Fermi), mitigated by fee-on-reserved-MW, Tesla ownership of all site assets, and step-in rights. Texas policy (SB6 curtailment, a governor-elect who campaigned on a data-center moratorium), mitigated by battery-backed controllable load and ISO diversification. Megapack supply, mitigated by the carve-out. Internal priority against a $20B capex budget, mitigated by presenting the build-versus-host comparison for a decision at day 45.

1. The thesis in one paragraph

Tesla needs a lot more training and inference compute and needs it before the end of 2027, but every new gigawatt of grid-connected load in the United States now waits three to seven years for interconnection. Bitcoin miners and a handful of power-first developers already hold more than 20 GW of energized or interconnection-approved capacity, and the mining business that justified it has collapsed to near breakeven. Hyperscalers and AI labs have spent eighteen months buying that capacity on fifteen- to twenty-year leases. Tesla has three advantages none of them has: it makes the battery every one of these sites needs to firm a curtailable interconnection, that battery is sold out for 2027, and it can show up with a self-contained compute-plus-storage module that removes the $10–12M-per-megawatt shell build from the deal. The program puts Tesla in as the tenant and offtaker at third-party sites, paying a fixed fee per reserved megawatt, bringing Megapods, and using Megapack allocation as the currency that closes.

2. Why now

Tesla's demand is public and immediate. Tesla told shareholders in October 2026 that it runs more than 225 MW of training compute at Giga Texas (Cortex 1 above 105 MW, Cortex 2 above 120 MW) and will reach 400 MW by year-end. 2026 capex is guided at roughly $20B and is explicitly weighted to "superintelligence compute infrastructure." The CEO's 2025 performance award vests on one million robotaxis in commercial operation, one million Optimus units delivered, and ten million paid FSD subscriptions, all of which are training-compute problems first. AI5 reaches volume in 2027 and Tesla has said spare AI5 silicon goes into Tesla data centers.

Tesla already defined the product. Tesla filed the MEGAPOD trademark in June 2026 for modular AI data center hardware in a Megapack-style enclosure. On the Q2 2026 call the CEO described Megapods as a way to "scale AI computing alongside aggregated power production" and talked about siting them at Superchargers. The Q3 2026 shareholder update says Megapack "excels in off-grid and on-grid applications, supplying large data center loads, buffering oscillating demand, helping interconnection grid-compliance." This program is the external version of that idea: Megapods where the power already is.

The grid is the constraint, and miners own the exits. ERCOT's large-load queue reached 474 GW in July 2026, about 90% of it data centers; PJM's large-load pipeline is in the hundreds of gigawatts; US average time-to-power for a new 100 MW+ load is around four years and seven in Northern Virginia; large transformers run 30–48 months. Modo Energy estimates only 7% of requested large load in ERCOT is deliverable by 2030. Meanwhile miners hold executed interconnection agreements and energized substations: Cipher alone has 807 MW energized and 3.2 GW with ERCOT conditional classifications; IREN energized 1.4 GW at Sweetwater in May 2026; Hut 8 has a 1 GW interconnection at Beacon Point; Core Scientific is expanding Pecos and Muskogee toward 1.5 GW gross each.

Mining economics have broken. Bitcoin at about $76,000, hashprice at $33 per PH per day (a five-year low), difficulty at a record, and a public-miner cash cost near $80,000 per coin mean roughly one in five legacy miners is losing money on every block. A megawatt that earns $0.2M a year mining earns $1.2–2.4M a year as HPC. Every miner CEO has said so on their last earnings call, and several (IREN, Keel, Cipher at Black Pearl) are shutting mining entirely.

The buyers are stretched and the sellers are nervous. Neoclouds have deployed only 25–30% of roughly $375B of contracted capacity. CoreWeave carries $51.6B of debt with bonds yielding above 16%; Microsoft said on Nov 6, 2026 that it will use fewer neoclouds. Nscale lost three Microsoft commitments this year. Crusoe paused and then sold a 1.8 GW Wyoming campus for lack of a tenant. Fermi America has 1.5 GW of turbines on the ground, one unclosed tenant, and lender deadlines in November and December. Keel Infrastructure (formerly Bitfarms) has 2.2 GW in development and zero signed leases. Texas elected a governor in November 2026 who campaigned on a data-center moratorium, which will make hosts with already-energized capacity more valuable and hosts with paper interconnections more motivated. An investment-grade tenant that funds its own build is the most valuable counterparty in this market, and today that role is played by Oracle, Microsoft, Anthropic, and Google. Tesla can be the fifth.

3. What Tesla is offering

The product. A Megapod block: roughly 20 MW of IT load in Tesla compute enclosures, paired with one or two Megablocks (20–40 MWh of Megapack 3 storage with integrated transformer and switchgear), on about an acre. Gross draw at the host's switchgear is about 26 MW. Five blocks make a 100 MW site. The battery does three jobs the host cares about: it smooths training-load swings so the utility never sees them, it rides through curtailment calls so the host's interconnection classification is protected, and it lets the site operate as a controllable load under Texas SB6 and ERCOT's batch rules. Block spec is a program assumption for Tesla Energy engineering to confirm.

The deal. Tesla is the tenant. The host keeps the land, the interconnection, the utility relationship, and site operations, and delivers energized gross megawatts at the point of interconnection. Tesla funds and owns everything from the switchgear in and pays a fixed site fee on reserved megawatts from the day power is available, with power passed through at cost. Ten-year initial term, two five-year Tesla options, Tesla parent guarantee. Equipment is a removable trade fixture.

The price. Opening at about $0.75M per gross MW-year, closing between $0.9M and $1.05M, walking away above $1.25M. That is three to five times the host's current mining margin on the same megawatt, with no shell to build. It is below the $1.8–1.9M market median for turnkey colocation because the host's capital is $1.5–3M per MW instead of $10–14M. The derivation is in economics and offer design.

The kicker. Tesla is the only tenant that also makes the asset the host needs. Four forms, used singly or in combination: a priority Megapack allocation for the host's own firming needs; battery-as-a-service where Tesla owns Megapacks the host leases; warrants, as Google took at Cipher and TeraWulf, AMD at Core Scientific, and Nvidia at IREN and Nebius; and a Tesla guarantee the host can finance against, as Hut 8 financed River Bend and Beacon Point with $7.5B of investment-grade project bonds.

Two variants. Variant A, the powered-land lease, for miners and landlords. Variant B, hosted Megapod services, for neoclouds that want to operate the site and sell Tesla power, network, and remote hands on a managed rate card. Both are in term sheet outline.

5. Why Tesla wins where others would not

  1. Tesla brings the data hall. No other tenant removes the shell from the host's balance sheet. That is why Tesla can pay less, start sooner, and still be the host's best offer.
  2. Tesla controls the scarce firming asset. Megapack allocation is a concession only Tesla can make. For a host with a Partial Capacity Load classification, it is worth more than the rent.
  3. Tesla is investment grade and pays from operating cash flow. Hosts' lenders have already financed billions against Anthropic, AWS, and Google-backstopped Fluidstack leases. Tesla paper finances the same way.
  4. Tesla is not a competitor to the host's other tenants. Tesla will not resell compute from these sites. Hosts can keep running hyperscaler processes on the rest of the campus.
  5. Tesla has done this at scale as a vendor. Roughly $1B of Megapacks are already firming AI training loads at Colossus in Memphis, bought by SpaceX. The engineering is proven; the program changes who owns the compute.

6. Sequencing

Wave 1 (first 45 days): Segment A, Texas and Oklahoma. Six first calls: Riot, Cipher, Core Scientific, Hut 8, Galaxy, Bitdeer. Objective: two term sheets on energized capacity with 2027 delivery. These are the fastest closes because the power exists and the miners' commercial leads (several hired in 2025–26 from CyrusOne, Prime Data Centers, Digital Realty) are measured on signings.

Wave 2 (days 30–75): Segment B and C, plus MARA and CleanSpark. Lead with the Megapack allocation at IREN, TeraWulf, Keel, and Fermi; the tenancy follows. Fermi's lender and landlord deadlines in November and December 2026 make it a special case: a 200–400 MW Tesla letter of intent could cure its covenants, which gives Tesla extraordinary leverage on terms and warrants, but also means doing diligence on a counterparty with going-concern language.

Wave 3 (days 60–90): Segment D. Nebius and Nscale first, because both have said publicly they want partners who finance, build, and operate. Crusoe second, noting that its board now includes the CEO of a competing storage supplier. CoreWeave last, because its stress is real but its leases are with the same landlords Tesla is already calling.

What not to do. Do not approach SpaceX or xAI capacity; the program must be arm's length from related parties after the November 2025 shareholder vote. Do not announce anything until the first term sheet is signed. Do not let Megapack sales to hosts' other capacity be gated on the tenancy; run them as separate orders through the standard Energy channel.

7. Governance inside Tesla

The program sits at the seam between Tesla Energy (Megapack, Megablock, site engineering, ERCOT registrations through Tesla Energy Ventures) and Tesla AI (compute roadmap, cluster operations). It needs one executive sponsor on each side, a Megapack allocation carve-out of roughly 2 GWh over 2027–28 that does not come out of signed customer backlog, a frozen reference-block datasheet, and a fee model with approval thresholds, all before the first call. Tesla Energy's leadership changed in October 2026, so the first internal step is confirming who owns Megapack commercially today. Details are in 90 day plan.

Tesla should contract on gross MW at the point of interconnection and let the Megapod's PUE be Tesla's problem.

Three consequences follow.

  1. The host's capex falls from $10–14M per MW to $1.5–3M per MW. That is why Tesla can and should pay less than $1.8M per MW-year. The host is renting power and dirt, not a data hall.

  3. Tesla owns ~80% of total capital at the site (compute plus battery) and all of the residual value. Megapods are trade fixtures and move if the site underperforms.

The decision is not about saving money. It is about buying three years and keeping optionality while Tesla's own chip roadmap (AI5 in 2027, AI6 and Terafab after) matures. Tesla is the only prospective tenant that also makes the firming asset the host needs. Megapack is reported as "almost sold out for 2027" with a record order backlog. That scarcity is a currency.

7. Why a host says yes

  1. Money. $0.75–1.05M per MW-year is three to five times current mining margin on the same megawatt, with no shell to build and no construction loan to raise.
  2. Speed. Revenue starts months, not years, after signing, because Tesla brings the data hall with it.
  3. Credit. Tesla is investment grade and pays from operating cash flow. Hosts' lenders finance against this.
  4. Firming. Megapack allocation turns curtailable megawatts into firm ones and keeps the host's remaining capacity marketable under Texas SB6 and ERCOT's batch process.
  5. Diversification. Core Scientific (CoreWeave), Riot (one AI lab), Hut 8 (two tenants), and IREN (Microsoft at 47% of backlog) all carry tenant-concentration flags from analysts.
  6. Optionality. Ten-year terms with removable equipment leave the host free to sign a 20-year hyperscaler lease on the rest of the campus.

8. Why Tesla says yes

  1. 400 MW+ of incremental training and inference capacity in 2027 without a second multi-year interconnection.
  2. Zero shell capex and no stranded real estate if the chip roadmap moves the compute elsewhere.
  3. Two GWh of internal Megapack demand per gigawatt of compute, plus a sales channel into hosts' remaining capacity.
  4. A productized, repeatable "Megapod site" that can later extend to Supercharger sites, as the CEO described on the Q2 2026 call.
  5. Grid diversification away from a single ERCOT node at Giga Texas.

Call scripts

Three scripts: the first live call to a decision-maker, the gatekeeper / executive-assistant call, and the second call once a non-disclosure agreement is in place. Written to be spoken, not read. Brackets mark where the caller substitutes the per-company hook from target list. Keep the first call under twelve minutes; the only goal is a 45-minute working session with their power and commercial leads.

Script A — First call to the CEO, President, or Chief Commercial / Data Center Officer

Open (30 seconds)

"[Name], this is [Caller] with Tesla Energy. I run [commercial / strategic sites] for the Megapack business and I'm calling about [Site], specifically the [X] megawatts you have [energized / coming online in 2027] that aren't under a long-term lease yet. I have a specific proposal and it takes about ten minutes to lay out. Is now bad?"

If "now is bad": "Understood. I'll send two paragraphs by email so you can decide whether it's worth fifteen minutes with your power and commercial leads. What's the best address for that?" Then stop. Do not pitch on a bad call.

Frame (60 seconds)

"The short version: Tesla wants to be a tenant, not a vendor. We'd bring our own compute and our own batteries to your site in a self-contained module we call a Megapod, and we'd pay you a fixed site fee on reserved megawatts, starting from the day power is available. You keep the interconnection, the land, and the operations. We bring everything from the switchgear in."

"Three things make that different from the AWS or CoreWeave leases you've seen. First, you don't build a data hall, so your capital is a couple of million per megawatt, not eleven or twelve. Second, we can energize months after signing instead of two years, because Megablock integrates the transformer and switchgear and we've deployed a gigawatt-hour in twenty business days. Third, you're getting Tesla credit on the paper, and you're getting a tenant that also makes the battery you need to firm the rest of your campus."

Discover (4–5 minutes)

Ask, then listen. Write down the numbers.

  1. "Walk me through what's actually uncontracted at [Site] today, in gross megawatts at the point of interconnection, and when each block is energized."
  2. "What's your interconnection classification? Firm, or [curtailable / Partial Capacity Load / conditional Base Load] under the ERCOT batch process?" (Outside Texas: "Any curtailment or demand-response obligations attached to the interconnection?")
  3. "What are you earning per megawatt on that capacity right now, mining or hosting, and what's your power cost?"
  4. "Have you got a tenant process running on it? Who's leading that on your side and where is it?"
  5. "If a tenant paid you a fixed fee per reserved megawatt and funded the entire build beyond the switchgear, what would you need per megawatt-year to prefer that over waiting for a fifteen-year hyperscaler lease?"
  6. "Who on your side owns power procurement and grid strategy? I'd like them on the next call."

Position (2 minutes)

Use their numbers. Example at a Texas site mining at 18 J/TH with $50 power:

"At today's hashprice you're clearing something like two hundred thousand dollars a megawatt-year before opex. We'd open in the high seven hundreds on reserved gross megawatts, escalating with CPI, and we'd fund everything past your switchgear. That's three to four times what the megawatt earns today, with no construction loan and no GPU risk on your books. And because we'd put Megapacks on the pad, we'd be happy to talk about a priority allocation for your own firming needs on the rest of the campus. Megapack is effectively sold out for 2027, so that allocation is worth something."

If they anchor on $1.8M+ colocation rent: "That's the right number for a landlord who funds a twelve-million-dollar-a-megawatt shell and waits two years for rent. We're asking you to fund two, and rent starts in months. The yield on your capital is higher in our structure. If you'd rather build the shell and have us lease it, we can price that too, but then we're in a hyperscaler process and we'd expect hyperscaler pricing."

Close (60 seconds)

"Here's what I'd propose. We sign a mutual NDA this week. You send me the one-line summary of each uncontracted block: gross megawatts, energization date, interconnection status, power cost. We come back inside ten business days with a site-specific term sheet, including the Megapack allocation option. Then we get your power lead and our Megapack engineering lead on a forty-five minute call to walk the pad layout. Does [day] or [day] next week work for that call?"

Confirm the attendees, confirm the NDA path, confirm the email address for the one-pager. Thank them. End the call.

Do not

  • Do not discuss Tesla's own compute roadmap, chip plans, or Giga Texas capacity beyond what is public.
  • Do not quote a walk-away number.
  • Do not mention SpaceX, xAI, or Colossus except to answer a direct question, and then only with public facts.
  • Do not disparage CoreWeave, Fluidstack, or any existing tenant.

Script B — Executive assistant or gatekeeper

"Good morning, this is [Caller] from Tesla Energy. I'm trying to reach [Name] about [Company]'s [Site] data center capacity. It's a commercial matter, not a sales call: Tesla is looking at leasing capacity at the site. Is [Name] the right person, or would that sit with [alternate name and title from the target list]?"

If asked to email: "Happy to. What's the best address, and is there a time [Name] usually takes outside calls? I'll keep it to two paragraphs."

If asked what Tesla wants: "We'd like to be a tenant at [Site]. We'd bring our own equipment and pay a fixed fee on reserved megawatts. I'd rather [Name] hear the numbers directly than through me paraphrasing, but that's the substance."

Ask for a calendar hold rather than a callback. Send the Email 1 template within the hour and reference the assistant by name.

Script C — Second call, after NDA, with the host's power and commercial leads

Agenda (state it up front, 30 seconds)

"Forty-five minutes. Three parts: you walk us through the blocks and the interconnection; we walk you through the Megapod reference block and how it sits on the pad; then we agree what goes into the term sheet and who signs what by when."

Part 1 — Their blocks (15 minutes)

Get on the record, block by block: gross MW at the point of interconnection; energization date; firm versus curtailable; utility or retail electric provider and current contract term and price; any existing tenant process, exclusivity, or right of first offer on the capacity; lender consents required; fiber providers at the site; water availability; acreage available for pads; any demand-response or 4CP programs the site participates in.

Part 2 — The reference block (15 minutes)

"Our reference Megapod block is about 20 megawatts of IT load with one to two Megablocks, which is 20 to 40 megawatt-hours of storage, on roughly an acre. Gross draw at the switchgear is about 26 megawatts. The battery does three jobs: it smooths the training load so your utility never sees the swings; it gives us ride-through so we can honor a curtailment call without dropping the job; and it lets us run as a controllable load, which protects your interconnection classification. Five blocks is a hundred megawatts and about five acres."

Then ask: "What does your interconnection agreement say about ramp rates and load factor? And what notice do you get on curtailment?"

Part 3 — Term sheet mechanics (15 minutes)

Agree on paper: reserved gross MW and delivery schedule by block; fee basis (reserved MW, not consumed energy) and the escalator; power pass-through mechanism; initial term and extension options; capacity-delivery liquidated damages; Tesla's expansion right of first refusal and the host's right of first offer on Tesla's next regional site; the Megapack allocation option and whether it is priced as a fee credit or a separate purchase order; credit support on both sides; who drafts (Tesla drafts the master site agreement; host drafts the site schedule).

Close

"We'll circulate the term sheet draft within ten business days of receiving your block data. Our ask is that you hold the [X] megawatts for thirty days while we paper it. Can you do that?"

"[Name], [Caller] with Tesla Energy. Calling about the uncontracted capacity at [Site]. Tesla would like to be a tenant there, bringing our own compute and batteries and paying a fixed fee per reserved megawatt. I've sent a short email with the specifics. [Number]. Thanks."

Objection handling

The objections hosts will raise, the ones Tesla's own executives will raise, and the answer to each. Answers are meant to be said out loud in one breath. Where the honest answer is "that is a real risk," it says so and gives the mitigation.

From hosts

"We're running a hyperscaler process. Why take less than $1.8M a megawatt?" Because you are not giving up $1.8M. That rent pays for a $12M-a-megawatt shell and two years of construction risk. In our structure you fund $2M and rent starts in months. Run the yield on your capital under both and ours is higher. And nothing stops you signing the hyperscaler lease on the rest of the campus; ten-year terms and removable equipment are the point.

"Tesla has never been a data center tenant. Why would you be a reliable one?" Tesla runs more than 225 MW of training compute at Giga Texas today and has said publicly it will reach 400 MW by year-end. The company has guided roughly $20B of 2026 capex at AI infrastructure, funded from operating cash flow. The lease would carry a Tesla, Inc. guarantee. Your lenders can underwrite that.

"We don't want a tenant that competes with us for Megapacks." The opposite. Tesla controls the allocation, and a Megapod site is the one place Tesla has a reason to put the host at the front of the line. The term sheet will include a priority allocation for your firming needs. No other tenant can offer that.

"Why a self-contained module? Our tenants want Tier III halls." Training compute does not need Tier III. It needs power, cooling, fiber, and ride-through, and the battery supplies the ride-through. The module is why your capital is $2M instead of $12M and why we can be running in months. If you want to build and lease a hall, we can price that, but then you are in a hyperscaler process on hyperscaler timelines.

"Our interconnection is curtailable / Partial Capacity Load / subject to SB6." That is exactly where this fits. With 20 to 40 MWh per block we can honor a curtailment call without dropping the job, run as a controllable load, and smooth the training swings your utility is worried about. A battery-backed tenant protects your classification; a bare GPU hall threatens it.

"Our lenders won't allow a new tenant without consent." We expect that. We will provide a direct agreement and non-disturbance language, and we will accept a security interest in the Tesla-funded site improvements. The River Bend and Beacon Point leases were financed with investment-grade project bonds; we will paper to that standard.

"What happens to the Megapods if Tesla walks?" They are trade fixtures. You have a purchase option at depreciated book value, we have a removal obligation with a decommissioning bond, and the termination fee steps down from 24 to 6 months of fees after year five. You are better protected than under a hall lease where the tenant leaves you a bespoke building.

"Why not just sell us Megapacks?" We will, and that conversation is separate. But a sale leaves your megawatts earning mining margin until a tenant arrives. The tenancy fills the megawatts now and brings the Megapacks with it.

"We've heard Tesla moves slowly on commercial agreements." Term sheet in ten business days of an NDA and the block data. We will put that in writing.

"What about Bitcoin's optics for Tesla?" Tesla holds Bitcoin on its balance sheet and has since 2021. The transaction is a lease of powered land and electrical capacity, not a mining venture. Optics are not a constraint for us.

From Tesla's own leadership

"Megapack is sold out for 2027. Why divert units to our own tenancy?" Because each 100 MW Megapod site pulls through 200 MWh of internal demand at cost and opens a sales channel into the host's remaining capacity, which is often several hundred more megawatts. The program needs a defined carve-out, roughly 2 GWh over two years, which is well under 2% of planned nameplate capacity once Megafactory Texas ramps.

"Why not just build at Giga Texas?" We are. Cortex 1 and 2 are at 225 MW and heading to 400 MW. The next 400 MW needs a new switchyard and more ERCOT interconnection at a time when the large-load queue is 474 GW. Hosting adds capacity in 2027 instead of 2029, on other people's interconnections, in several ISOs, with no stranded shell if the chip roadmap moves the compute.

"These counterparties are leveraged crypto miners." Some are. That is why the structure is a site fee on reserved MW with Tesla owning every asset from the switchgear in, step-in rights with the utility, and non-disturbance from the host's lenders. If a host fails, Tesla keeps the equipment and the power, and the site has a replacement landlord within weeks. The pattern is proven: Google, AMD, and Nvidia have all taken warrants and provided backstops to these same companies.

"Isn't this what SpaceX and xAI do? Why would Tesla do it independently?" SpaceX sells compute to third parties from Memphis. Tesla needs compute for FSD, Optimus, and robotaxi. This program is Tesla-only, at arm's length, with no related-party element, which is what the board and shareholders asked for after the November 2025 vote.

"Why not partner with a neocloud and rent GPUs?" GPU cloud costs run roughly $9–10M per MW-year all-in (IREN–Microsoft). Hosting our own compute at $1M per MW-year in site fees plus owned hardware is cheaper over any horizon longer than two years, and it keeps the hardware roadmap in our hands.

"The Submer 'MegaPod' trademark is a problem." Possibly, for the marketing name. It is not a problem for a site agreement, which can describe the equipment generically. Legal should clear the naming before any public announcement.

"What if ERCOT or Texas changes the rules again?" That is a real risk, which is why the target list spans ERCOT, SPP, MISO, PJM, and NYISO, and why the battery is in every block. Texas SB6 already gives ERCOT curtailment authority over large loads; a battery-backed, controllable load is the design that survives whatever comes next.

Term sheet outline

Non-binding structure for a Tesla-as-offtaker Megapod deployment at a partner (Bitcoin miner or neocloud) site. Two variants are offered; counsel to convert into a Master Site Agreement (MSA) with site-specific schedules.

Parties and roles

Role Party Obligations
Site Partner ("Host") Miner / neocloud entity holding the interconnection agreement, land, and (where applicable) shells Deliver energized capacity (MW at the point of interconnection), land/pad, fiber route, water (if any), security, 24/7 site operations
Offtaker / Tenant Tesla, Inc. (or a Tesla Energy subsidiary) Fund, deliver, install, and operate Megapods (Megapack 3 / Megablock storage + containerized compute); pay site fees; consume power
Power counterparty Host's utility / retail electric provider / ERCOT QSE Deliver energy under Host's existing supply arrangements, or assigned to Tesla under a back-to-back supply agreement

Variant A — "Powered Land Lease" (preferred for miners)

  1. Premises: defined pad(s) sized for N Megapods, plus easements for switchgear, fiber, and access. Triple-net ground lease.
  2. Delivered capacity: Host guarantees X MW of firm interconnection capacity available to the Premises by the Capacity Delivery Date, with liquidated damages for late delivery (fee abatement 1:1 plus a daily LD after a 60-day grace period).
  3. Term: 10 years initial + two 5-year Tesla extension options. Tesla early-termination right after year 5 with 18 months' notice and a termination fee stepping down from 24 to 6 months of site fees.
  4. Site fee: fixed $/MW-month on reserved capacity (not consumed energy), with CPI-linked escalator capped at 3%/yr. Indicative range to be set against colocation comps in economics and offer design (Tesla self-funds the Megapods, so the fee should sit well below turnkey colocation rents).
  5. Power pass-through: energy at Host's actual cost plus a transparent, capped adder (or Tesla takes assignment of the supply contract). Tesla keeps 100% of battery-derived grid revenue (ancillary services, 4CP avoidance, demand response) unless the Host co-invests.
  6. Flexibility covenant: Tesla commits to curtail compute load within ERCOT/utility notice windows, using Megapack discharge first, so the Host keeps any interruptible-load tariff benefits.
  7. Expansion: Tesla ROFR on the next Y MW the Host energizes at the site and at named sister sites; Host ROFO to host Tesla's next regional deployment.
  8. Credit support: Tesla parent guarantee (investment-grade). Host provides a security interest in site improvements paid for by Tesla and a non-disturbance agreement from Host's lenders.
  9. Operations: Host provides physical security, grounds, and first-response; Tesla operates Megapods remotely with on-call Tesla Energy field service. Shared SLA with uptime credits on delivered capacity.
  10. Exit: Megapods are removable trade fixtures; Tesla may remove or sell to Host at fair value at expiry. Host has purchase option on the Megapack units at depreciated book value.

Variant B — "Hosted Compute Services" (preferred for neoclouds)

  1. Service: Host operates Tesla-owned Megapods inside its campus and sells Tesla power, cooling (where used), network, and remote-hands as a managed colocation service.
  2. Pricing: $/kW-month on installed IT capacity plus metered power at cost; Tesla self-provides compute hardware, so the rate card excludes GPU margin.
  3. Term: 5–7 years, matched to Host's power contract term; renewal at then-market.
  4. Performance: tiered SLA (99.9% on power delivered to Megapod bus; 99.99% where Megapack ride-through is in the loop) with service credits.
  5. Compute interoperability: Tesla racks may be Tesla-designed (AI5/AI6) or merchant GPU; Host network must support Tesla's interconnect spec.
  6. Battery services option: Host may lease Megapack capacity from Tesla for its own ride-through or grid-revenue purposes at a fixed $/MW-month, netting against site fees.
  7. Credit and exit: as Variant A, with step-in rights for Tesla if Host becomes insolvent (direct agreement with Host's utility and landlord).

Common schedules

  • Schedule 1: Site description, interconnection agreement summary, capacity delivery milestones
  • Schedule 2: Megapod specification (unit count, MWh storage, MW compute, footprint, cooling, fiber)
  • Schedule 3: Fee schedule and escalators
  • Schedule 4: SLA and curtailment protocol (ERCOT/utility notice windows, Megapack discharge priority)
  • Schedule 5: Insurance, environmental, decommissioning
  • Schedule 6: Transition / step-in plan

Deal-protection points Tesla should hold firm on

  • Reserved-capacity pricing (never tied to Tesla's compute revenue).
  • Tesla owns all battery grid revenue by default.
  • Capacity delivery LDs backed by escrow or a parent guarantee from the Host.
  • No exclusivity granted to the Host beyond the specific site(s).
  • Most-favored-nation on site fees versus any other tenant on the same campus.
